\begin{abstract}
Let $\gamma(n)$ be defined by
\[
  \xi\!\left(\frac12+w\right)
  =\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\gamma(n)}{n!}w^{2n},
\qquad
  \J^{d,n}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom dj\gamma(n+j)X^j.
\]
We prove that there is an absolute constant $K>0$ such that
$n^2\log(n+2)\ge Kd^3$ implies that $\J^{d,n}$ has $d$ distinct
negative real zeros.  The exponent $2/3$ comes from matching six consecutive
coefficients with a four-parameter product of two Jacobi factors and then
bounding the sixth interpolation remainder.  The proof combines a direct
sectorial saddle analysis of the xi coefficients, finite-free convolution,
a non-perturbative hypergeometric recurrence, and complex
Hermite--Genocchi interpolation.  The argument does not prove the Riemann
hypothesis and does not locate zeros of $\zeta(s)$.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and theorem}\label{sec:introduction}

The Jensen polynomials attached to a sequence $(a(n))_{n\ge0}$ are
\[
  J_a^{d,n}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom dj a(n+j)X^j.
\]
When $a(n)=\gamma(n)$ comes from Riemann's xi-function, hyperbolicity of
these polynomials is a finite shadow of the real-zero geometry of the
completed function.  Griffin, Ono, Rolen, and Zagier proved eventual
hyperbolicity for each fixed degree and established a Hermite limit
\cite{GORZ2019}; Griffin, Ono, Rolen, Thorner, Tripp, and Wagner developed
the xi-specific asymptotics \cite{GORTTW2020}.  Holland introduced a
finite-free comparison architecture that yields a degree growing with the
shift \cite{Holland2026}.  The present argument changes the local comparison
from a five-match to a six-match model and replaces the portion of the old
localization argument whose small-gap hypothesis fails on the new branch.

\begin{theorem}[Two-thirds hyperbolicity wedge]\label{thm:main}
There is an absolute constant $K>0$ such that, for all integers $d\ge1$ and
$n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wedge}
       n^2\log(n+2)\ge Kd^3
\end{equation}
implies that $\J^{d,n}$ has exactly $d$ distinct negative real zeros.
\end{theorem}

The proof is developed in Sections~\ref{sec:mellin}--\ref{sec:assembly} and
is closed in an explicit \emph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}} in
Section~\ref{sec:assembly}.  The intermediate coefficient theorem,
Theorem~\ref{thm:sector}, is likewise followed immediately by its proof.

The constant is effective in the usual proof-theoretic sense: every finite
geometric contribution is explicit, while two analytic constants can be
read from the sectorial saddle proof.  The direct unweighted sup-norm
contraction is extremely wasteful, so we make no claim that the resulting
constant is useful for computation.  In particular, the numerical root
examples in the verification package are checks, not the finite verification
of an optimized threshold.

\subsection{What the theorem does not say}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is a statement about finite polynomials formed from
Taylor coefficients of $\xi$.  It neither proves that the nontrivial zeros
of $\zeta$ lie on the critical line nor sharpens a zero-free region.  It also
does not say that every Jensen polynomial is hyperbolic; it gives a
sufficient asymptotic wedge.  The theorem's scale is
$d\ll(n^2\log n)^{1/3}$, hence the name ``two-thirds wedge'' when the
relation is expressed as a lower bound on $n$ in terms of $d$.

\subsection{Architecture}

The proof has six interfaces.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The centered xi coefficients are expressed as a positive Mellin moment
with the exact factor-eight normalization.
\item A sectorial saddle theorem gives an explicit nonzero main term and a
holomorphic relative error, which can be differentiated six times.
\item Four quotient coordinates and two normalizations select a positive
four-parameter hypergeometric comparison matching six coefficients.
\item Jacobi factorization and finite-free convolution give positive, simple,
localized comparison roots.
\item A direct shifted ${}_3F_2$ recurrence bounds all derivatives at every
critical point without treating the surviving gap parameter as small.
\item Complex six-node interpolation bounds the coefficient multiplier by
$d^3/(n^2\log n)$, after which sign transfer finishes the proof.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Definitions and normalization}\label{sec:definitions}

We use
\[
 \xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s).
\]
It is entire and satisfies $\xi(1-s)=\xi(s)$.  We work with the complex
centered variable $w$, not the classical real variable $t$ in
$\Xi(t)=\xi(1/2+it)$.  Thus no hidden power of $i$ enters the coefficient
sequence.

\begin{definition}[Xi coefficients and Jensen polynomial]
The real coefficients $\gamma(n)$ and the Jensen polynomial are defined by
\begin{align}
 \xi(1/2+w)&=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{\gamma(n)}{n!}w^{2n},
 \label{eq:centeredxi}\\
 \J^{d,n}(X)&=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom dj\gamma(n+j)X^j.
 \label{eq:jensen}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

The functional equation makes the left side of \eqref{eq:centeredxi} even.
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taylor-normalization}
 \gamma(n)=\frac{n!}{(2n)!}
 \left.\frac{\dd^{2n}}{\dd w^{2n}}\xi(1/2+w)\right|_{w=0}.
\end{equation}
This normalization is load-bearing.  The two most dangerous transcription
errors are replacing $n!$ by $2^{2n}$ and replacing the kernel weight
$e^{u/2}$ below by $e^u$; both give plausible-looking but false formulas.

\subsection{Comparison variables}

Write
\[
 x=n^{-1},\qquad e=\Lm_n^{-1},\qquad \Lm_n=L_{2n-2},
\]
where $L_N$ is the saddle variable defined in
Section~\ref{sec:saddle}.  For positive parameters
$y=(\alpha,t,w,\delta)$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABCD}
 A=\frac{\alpha}{xe},\qquad
 B=\frac{t+we}{x},\qquad
 C=\frac{t}{x},\qquad
 D=\frac{1+\delta e}{x}.
\end{equation}
The comparison polynomial is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-def}
 p_F(y_0)={}_3F_2\!\left(
 \begin{matrix}-d,A,C\\B,D\end{matrix};
 \frac{D}{AC}y_0\right).
\end{equation}
The independent variable is denoted $y_0$ in this display to distinguish it
from the parameter vector $y$.

\section{Prior work and precise novelty}\label{sec:prior}

GORZ expand logarithmic coefficient ratios and obtain a Hermite limit for
each fixed degree \cite{GORZ2019}.  GORTTW supply the xi moment asymptotics
needed when the degree varies \cite{GORTTW2020}.  Holland replaces a local
Hermite perturbation by a hypergeometric comparison formed with finite-free
convolution \cite{Holland2026}.  We retain three parts of that architecture:
a low-dimensional matching system, positive-rooted Jacobi factors, and sign
stability at critical points.

The change from the earlier three-fifths scale to \eqref{eq:wedge} is local
and exact.  Matching six coefficients makes the logarithmic ratio vanish at
six consecutive nodes.  The six-node Hermite--Genocchi remainder contains
six factors, and the critical-point radius is $\rho\asymp\sqrt{nd}$.
Therefore
\[
 \frac{\rho^6}{n^5\log n}
 \asymp \frac{(nd)^3}{n^5\log n}
 =\frac{d^3}{n^2\log n}.
\]
The wedge is precisely the condition that this quantity be small.

Three repairs are essential rather than cosmetic.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The exact xi/Mellin identity is derived directly, because two printed
coefficient conventions differ by a factor of eight.
\item Holland's assembled localization lemma is not imported: its
$(C-D)/D\le1/4$ hypothesis fails on the new branch, where the quotient tends
to $1$.
\item The shifted hypergeometric recurrence retains
$\epsilon_p=(C-D)/C$, which tends to $1/2$ and is not perturbative.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting proof shares an architecture with the cited work but not a
black-box dependence on its headline conclusion.

\section{Exact xi/Mellin identity}\label{sec:mellin}

Let
\[
 \Theta(t)=\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-\pi m^2t},\qquad
 \omega(t)=\frac12\bigl(2t^2\Theta''(t)+3t\Theta'(t)\bigr).
\]
Riemann's integral representation, integration by parts, and the change of
variables $t=e^{2u}$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi-kernel}
 \xi(1/2+w)=8\int_0^\infty
  \omega(e^{2u})e^{u/2}\cosh(wu)\,\dd u.
\end{equation}
The kernel is positive for $u>0$, decays superexponentially as
$u\to+\infty$, and extends to an even full-line kernel by the modular
transformation of the theta function.  These properties justify termwise
differentiation on compact $w$-sets.

\begin{proposition}[Factor-eight coefficient identity]\label{prop:factor8}
For every integer $n\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor8}
 \gamma(n)=\frac{8n!}{(2n)!}
 \int_0^\infty\omega(e^{2u})e^{u/2}u^{2n}\,\dd u.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Expand $\cosh(wu)=\sum_{n\ge0}(wu)^{2n}/(2n)!$ in
\eqref{eq:xi-kernel}.  Dominated convergence on compact $w$-sets permits
coefficient extraction.  Comparing with \eqref{eq:centeredxi} gives
\eqref{eq:factor8}.  The duplication formula yields the equivalent prefactor
\[
 \frac{8n!}{(2n)!}=\frac{8\sqrt\pi}{4^n\Gamma(n+1/2)}.
\]
\end{proof}

Expanding $\Theta$ mode by mode introduces an auxiliary Mellin moment $F(s)$.
With $N=2M-2$, direct algebra gives the exact continued identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moment-assembly}
 \gamma(M)=\frac{\Gamma(M+1)}{\Gamma(2M+1)}2^{-2M-2}
 \left(32\binom{2M}{2}F(2M-2)-F(2M)\right).
\end{equation}
It is initially obtained in a right half-plane where the integrals converge
absolutely and then continued holomorphically.  The relation
$32\binom{N+2}{2}=16(N+2)(N+1)$ will be used in the saddle assembly.
For later use define the continued moment
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mz-definition}
 M_z:=2^{-2z-2}\left(32\binom{2z}{2}F(2z-2)-F(2z)\right).
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:moment-assembly} is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma-M}
 \gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{\Gamma(2z+1)}M_z.
\end{equation}

\section{Quantitative sectorial saddle}\label{sec:saddle}

Fix principal logarithms in a sector $|\arg N|\le\theta<\pi/2$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:saddle-eq}
 N=L\left(\pi e^L+\frac34\right)=:\Psi(L).
\end{equation}
Put $\ell=\log|N|$ and
\[
 L_0=\log N-\log\log N-\log\pi.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Sectorial saddle branch]\label{lem:saddle}
For $|N|\ge e^{12}$ in a fixed sector of angle less than $\pi/2$, there is a
unique zero $L_N$ of \eqref{eq:saddle-eq} in $|L-L_0|<1$.  These zeros patch
to a holomorphic branch agreeing with the positive real solution.  On every
fixed smaller closed sector,
\begin{gather}
 |L_N|\asymp\log|N|,\qquad
 r=L_N^{-1}\to0,\qquad \sigma=L_N/N\to0,\label{eq:rsigma}\\
 |L_N|\ge\tfrac12\log|N|,\qquad |r|,|\sigma|\le\frac7{50},\label{eq:box-rsigma}\\
 Q_N=(1+L_N)N-\tfrac34L_N^2
 =NL_N(1+r-\tfrac34\sigma),\label{eq:Q}\\
 |1+r-\tfrac34\sigma|\ge\frac9{16},\qquad
 L_N'=\frac{L_N}{Q_N}.
 \label{eq:Lprime}
\end{gather}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
On $D_N=\{L:|L-L_0|\le1\}$ consider
\[
 G_N(L)=L+\log L+\log\pi
 -\log\left(1-\frac{3L}{4N}\right)-\log N.
\]
Let
\[
 m(\ell)=\ell-\log(\ell+1)-\log\pi-1,
\quad
 M_\theta(\ell)=\ell+2+\log(\ell+1)+\theta/\ell+\log\pi.
\]
For $L\in D_N$, direct geometry gives
$\Re L\ge m(\ell)>0$ and $|L|\le M_\theta(\ell)$.  Hence every logarithm is
legal, and $|3L/(4N)|<10^{-4}$ when $\ell\ge12$.

On $\partial D_N$, compare $G_N$ with $H_N(L)=L-L_0$.  The inequality
$|\log(1+z)|\le2|z|$ for $|z|\le1/2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rouche-sum}
 |G_N-H_N|
 \le 2\frac{\log(\ell+1)+\theta/\ell+\log\pi+1}{\ell}
 +\frac{3M_\theta(\ell)}{2e^\ell}<1.
\end{equation}
Rouch\'e's theorem yields one zero, counted with multiplicity.  Exponentiating
$G_N(L_N)=0$ gives \eqref{eq:saddle-eq}.  The derivative of the left side of
\eqref{eq:saddle-eq} is $Q_N/L_N$.  Before invoking the implicit-function
theorem, use \eqref{eq:box-rsigma} and the reverse triangle inequality:
\[
 |1+r-\tfrac34\sigma|
 \ge1-\frac7{50}-\frac34\frac7{50}
 =\frac{151}{200}>\frac9{16}.
\]
Thus $Q_N\ne0$, the zero is simple, and local holomorphic branches agree on
overlaps by uniqueness.  Conjugation symmetry and uniqueness on the positive
axis select the required branch.  The disc bounds also give
$m(\ell)>\ell/2$ and the limits in \eqref{eq:rsigma}.  Implicit
differentiation now gives \eqref{eq:Lprime}.
\end{proof}

The reduced denominator needed at order six has the stronger explicit bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced-denominator}
 |4+4r-3\sigma|\ge4-4\frac7{50}-3\frac7{50}=\frac{151}{50}.
\end{equation}

\section{Contour deformation and leading mode}\label{sec:contour}

For the leading theta mode, write the Mellin integral in $u=\log t$ and keep
the Jacobian outside the phase so that the saddle remains
\eqref{eq:saddle-eq}.  Explicitly, set
\[
 \Phi_s(u)=s\operatorname{Log}u-\frac34u-\pi e^u,
 \qquad g_s(u)=e^u e^{\Phi_s(u)},
 \qquad I_1(s):=F_1(s):=\int_0^\infty g_s(u)\,\dd u.
\]
Write $L_s=\alpha+ib$.  Split the original ray at
$u=1$ and apply Cauchy's theorem to the rectangle with vertices
$1,X,X+ib,1+ib$.  The right connector tends to zero as $X\to\infty$, giving
the exact identity
\[
 I_1(s)=E_s+\int_1^\infty g_s(x+ib)\,\dd x,
 \qquad
 E_s=\int_0^1g_s(x)\,\dd x+i\int_0^b g_s(1+iy)\,\dd y.
\]
Thus only the ray $[1,\infty)$ is translated.  It remains in $\Re u\ge1$,
away from the principal-log cut.  On this actual ray put $u=L_s+r$, so
$r\ge1-\Re L_s$; the expression $L_s+iv$ would be vertical and would
reverse the quadratic sign.  The exact phase difference has quadratic part
$-K_sr^2/2$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature}
 K_s=\frac{s}{L_s}\left(1+\frac1{L_s}-\frac{3L_s}{4s}\right),
 \qquad Q_s=L_s^2K_s.
\end{equation}
The direct imaginary-part bootstrap from the saddle equation gives
$|\arg K_s|<1/20$, and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature-positive}
 \Re K_s\ge\cos(1/20)|K_s|>0.
\end{equation}

Choose a central window $|r|\le |K_s|^{-2/5}$.  It lies on the actual ray for
all sufficiently large $|s|$.  Taylor's theorem with an integral remainder
gives
\[
 \Phi_s(L_s+r)-\Phi_s(L_s)
 =-\frac12K_sr^2+\frac16\Phi_s^{(3)}(L_s)r^3+R_4(s,r),
\]
with $|R_4(s,r)|\le C|K_s||r|^4$ on the fixed sector.  The Jacobian
outside the phase contributes the exact amplitude $e^r$.  After the omitted
left Gaussian tail has been bounded exponentially, the full real Gaussian is
used only as a comparison integral.  Completing the square gives
\[
 \frac{\int_{\R}r^3e^{r-K_sr^2/2}\,\dd r}
 {\int_{\R}e^{r-K_sr^2/2}\,\dd r}
 =\frac3{K_s^2}+\frac1{K_s^3}.
\]
Since $\Phi_s^{(3)}(L_s)=O(|K_s|)$, the signed cubic therefore contributes
$O(|K_s|^{-1})$ relatively; estimating its absolute value first would lose
this order.  Equation
\eqref{eq:curvature-positive} dominates the central integrand by a real
Gaussian, so dominated convergence is uniform.

Outside the central window, strict concavity on the actual shifted ray gives
an increasing left tail on $1\le x\le\alpha-V$ and an exponentially
decreasing right tail on $x\ge\alpha+V$, where $V=|K_s|^{-2/5}$.  The two
tail integrals are $O(e^{-c|K_s|^{1/5}})$ relative to the saddle main term.
The endpoint term $E_s$ satisfies the stronger
$O(e^{-c|s|\log\log|s|})$ relative bound.  Consequently the leading moment has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leading-moment}
 F_1(s)=\mathcal A(s)
 \left(1+O\left(\frac1{|K_s|}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-main}
 \mathcal A(s)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{K_s}}L_s^s
 \exp\!\left(\frac{L_s}{4}-\frac{s}{L_s}+\frac34\right).
\end{equation}
All roots and logarithms in \eqref{eq:A-main} are continued from the positive
axis.  The detailed connector parametrizations and numerical-free constants
are recorded in the technical supplement.

\section{Higher theta modes and coefficient theorem}\label{sec:coefficient}

For $N=2M-2$, define the candidate coefficient main term by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G-main}
 \Gm(M)=
 \frac{e^{M-2}M^{M+1/2}L_N^N}
 {2^{2M-2}N^{N+1/2}}
 \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{K_N}}
 \exp\!\left(\frac{L_N}{4}-\frac{N}{L_N}+\frac34\right),
\end{equation}
where all powers and the square root are continued from the positive axis.

\begin{theorem}[Sectorial coefficient asymptotic]\label{thm:sector}
Let $\theta_0=1/400$ and $\theta_1=1/200$.  There are $R,C>0$ such that
$\Gm$ is holomorphic and nonzero on
\[
 |M|>R,\qquad |\arg M|<\theta_1,
\]
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sector-asymptotic}
 \gamma(M)=\Gm(M)(1+\Em(M)),
 \qquad
 |\Em(M)|\le C\frac{\log|M|}{|M|}
\end{equation}
on the closed inner sector $|\arg M|\le\theta_0$.
The error $\Em$ is holomorphic on the outer sector.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The $m$th theta mode has the same analytic contour and an extra exponential
term proportional to $-(m^2-1)e^{L_s+r}$.  On the central window its real
part is at most $-c(m^2-1)|s|/|L_s|$; on the tails the leading concavity
estimate already dominates.  Summation over $m\ge2$ is therefore
exponentially small compared with \eqref{eq:leading-moment}.  This conclusion
is uniform on the fixed inner sector, not uniform in an arbitrary free sector
angle.

Insert \eqref{eq:leading-moment} into \eqref{eq:moment-assembly}.  A two-step
Taylor expansion of $\log\mathcal A(s)$ controls $F(N+2)/F(N)$, and sectorial
Stirling controls the gamma ratio.  The cancellation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logA-derivative}
\frac{\dd}{\dd s}\log\mathcal A(s)
 =\log L_s+\frac1{L_sK_s}-\frac12\frac{K_s'}{K_s}
\end{equation}
uses the saddle equation exactly.  With $N=2M-2$ the main term is
\eqref{eq:G-main}.  More explicitly, Taylor's formula on the segment from
$N$ to $N+2$ and the bound for the second derivative of
$\log\mathcal A$ give
\[
 \frac{F(N+2)}{F(N)}=L_N^2
 \left(1+O\left(\frac{\log|N|}{|N|}\right)\right).
\]
Combining this ratio with \eqref{eq:moment-assembly}, the leading-mode
estimate \eqref{eq:leading-moment}, the exponentially small sum of the
higher modes, and sectorial Stirling yields \eqref{eq:sector-asymptotic}.

The shrink from the leading-mode sector ensures that $N=2M-2$ remains in
the required domain after increasing $R$.  The fixed angles also leave room
for proportional Cauchy discs around points in the inner sector.  The
saddle lemma and \eqref{eq:curvature-positive} make every factor of
\eqref{eq:G-main} holomorphic and nonzero there after continuation from the
positive axis.  Hence the quotient defining $\Em$ is holomorphic on the
outer sector; the preceding uniform estimates give the asserted bound on
the closed inner sector.  Appendix~D records the connector, central-window,
tail, mode-summation, and two-step-ratio estimates separately.

The accompanying Lean theorem follows these quantifiers literally.  It uses
the wider proof sector $|\arg s|<1/100$ to transport $N=2M-2$, proves
holomorphy and nonvanishing on $|\arg M|<1/200$, and proves the displayed
rate on $|\arg M|\le 1/400$, with explicit witnesses for $R$ and $C$.
Its Cauchy corollary transports the resulting error through order six.
\end{proof}

\section{Logarithmic derivatives through order six}\label{sec:derivatives}

By \eqref{eq:gamma-M}, Theorem~\ref{thm:sector}, and the nonvanishing of the
gamma function, $M_z$ is nonzero after increasing the sectorial threshold.
Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h-definition}
 h(z):=\log M_z
\end{equation}
on the branch real on the positive axis.  This is the logarithm of the
auxiliary moment, not $\log\gamma(z)$.  The exact bridge is
\[
 \log\gamma(z)=h(z)+\log\Gamma(z+1)-\log\Gamma(2z+1),
\]
with branches continued from positivity.  Theorem~\ref{thm:sector} supplies
a holomorphic relative error on a larger sector.  If a disc of radius $R_x$
around $x$ remains in that sector and $|\Em|\le\epsilon_x$ on its boundary,
Cauchy's estimate gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cauchy-transport}
 |\Em^{(j)}(x)|\le\frac{j!\epsilon_x}{R_x^j}.
\end{equation}
Taking $R_x$ proportional to $x$ transfers the error in
\eqref{eq:sector-asymptotic} through every derivative needed below.

Before differentiating, convert the coefficient main term to the
auxiliary-moment main term by subtracting the explicit gamma factors in the
exact bridge:
\[
 \log\Gm(x)-\log\Gamma(x+1)+\log\Gamma(2x+1).
\]
After the sectorial Stirling cancellations already used to obtain
\eqref{eq:G-main}, its moving-saddle logarithm is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:G0-definition}
 G_0(N)=(N+1)\log L_N+\frac{L_N}{4}-\frac{N}{L_N}
 -\frac12\log Q_N,
\end{equation}
with logarithms continued from the positive axis.  Put $N=2x-2$,
$r=L_N^{-1}$, and $\sigma=L_N/N$.  Total implicit differentiation of this
moment saddle term, including the chain $N=2x-2$, yields
\begin{align}
 h''(x)&=\frac{2}{xL_N}\bigl(1+O(L_N^{-1})\bigr),\nonumber\\
 h^{(3)}(x)&=-\frac{2}{x^2L_N}\bigl(1+O(L_N^{-1})\bigr),\nonumber\\
 h^{(4)}(x)&=\frac{4}{x^3L_N}\bigl(1+O(L_N^{-1})\bigr),\label{eq:derivative-tower}\\
 h^{(5)}(x)&=-\frac{12}{x^4L_N}\bigl(1+O(L_N^{-1})\bigr),\nonumber\\
 h^{(6)}(x)&=\frac{48}{x^5L_N}\bigl(1+O(L_N^{-1})\bigr).\nonumber
\end{align}
Before the chain rule, the fifth and sixth leading derivatives have constants
$-6$ and $24$; after $N=2x-2$ they become $-12$ and $48$.  Keeping both
stages visible prevents a factor-two ambiguity.

Its exact sixth derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H6-form}
 G_0^{(6)}(N)=\frac{H_6(r,\sigma)}{N^5L_N},
 \qquad
 H_6(r,\sigma)=\frac{P_{13}(r,\sigma)}{(4+4r-3\sigma)^{12}},
\end{equation}
where $P_{13}$ has total degree $13$ and $82$ nonzero monomials.  Exact
coefficientwise majorization on $|r|,|\sigma|\le7/50$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H6-majorant}
 |H_6(r,\sigma)|\le
 \frac{6422139805764931584036533551104}
 {702576099728137594188684005}<10^4.
\end{equation}
Together with \eqref{eq:reduced-denominator}, this yields the deliberately
coarse bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h6-bound}
 |G_0^{(6)}(N)|\le\frac{20000}{|N|^5\log|N|}.
\end{equation}

a decimal fit for any constant in \eqref{eq:derivative-tower}.

\section{Six matches and quotient coordinates}\label{sec:matches}

The comparison coefficients are chosen through four consecutive quotient
coordinates.  If $(a_j)$ is a nonzero sequence with compatible logarithms,
define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quotient-coordinate}
 q_k(a)=\log a_{k+2}-2\log a_{k+1}+\log a_k,
 \qquad 0\le k\le3.
\end{equation}
These are logarithms of the ratios
$a_{k+2}a_k/a_{k+1}^2$.

\begin{lemma}[Four quotients plus two normalizations]\label{lem:sixmatch}
Suppose $a_j,b_j\ne0$ for $0\le j\le5$, logarithms have been chosen
compatibly, $q_k(a)=q_k(b)$ for $0\le k\le3$, and
$a_0=b_0$, $a_1=b_1$.  Then $a_j=b_j$ for $0\le j\le5$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set $c_j=\log a_j-\log b_j$.  The hypotheses say $c_0=c_1=0$ and
$c_{k+2}=2c_{k+1}-c_k$ for $0\le k\le3$.  Induction gives
$c_2=\cdots=c_5=0$.  Exponentiation gives the result.
\end{proof}

For the xi coefficients and the hypergeometric model, the four equations are
written as a residual map $G_n(\alpha,t,w,\delta)=0$.  The logarithmic gamma
terms are decomposed before estimating.  For
\[
 f_k(U)=\log(U+k)-\log(U+k+1),
\]
the exact half-shift is $-f_k(n+1/2)$.  Pairing it with the nearby $D$ term
prevents an unnecessary factor of $L_n$.

\section{Elementary cube calculus}\label{sec:cube}

For $q=j+1$, repeated real FTC gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cube-ftc}
 \Delta^jf_0(U)=(-1)^{j+1}j!
 \int_{[0,1]^q}(U+u_1+\cdots+u_q)^{-q}\,\dd u.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi}
 \Phi_q(s,z)=\int_{[0,1]^q}
 (s+z(u_1+\cdots+u_q))^{-q}\,\dd u.
\end{equation}
The finite-dimensional cube has volume one.  If $s\ge s_0>0$ and $z\ge0$,
differentiation under the integral gives, for $r=0,1,2$,
\begin{align}
 \partial_s^r\Phi_q(s,z)
 &=(-1)^r(q)_r\int_{[0,1]^q}
 (s+z\textstyle\sum u_i)^{-q-r}\,\dd u,
 \label{eq:Phi-derivative}\\
 |\partial_s^r\Phi_q(s,z)|&\le(q)_rs_0^{-q-r}.
 \label{eq:Phi-bound}
\end{align}
The mean-value theorem yields the explicit first-order comparison
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi-error}
 \left|\partial_s^r\Phi_q(s,z)-(-1)^r(q)_rs^{-q-r}\right|
 \le(q)_r(q+r)qz\,s_0^{-q-r-1}.
\end{equation}

The normalized elementary part of the four-coordinate residual is
\begin{align}
 H_{n,j}=\frac{a_j}{e}\{&e^q\Phi_q(\alpha,xe)
 -\Phi_q(t+we,x)+\Phi_q(t,x)\nonumber\\
 &-\Phi_q(1+\delta e,x)+\Phi_q(1+x/2,x)\},
 \label{eq:Hnj}
\end{align}
with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aj}
 (a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3)=(1,1/2,1,1).
\end{equation}

We record the three different limiting mechanisms, since conflating them is
a common source of a false scale.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The remote $A$ boundary contains the external factor $e^q/e$ and
therefore contributes $1/\alpha$ only for $q=1$.
\item The $B-C$ divided difference has length $we$ and tends to
$qw/t^{q+1}$.
\item The $D$--gamma half-shift pair has length $\delta e-x/2$ after the
correct rescaling and tends to $q\delta$.
\end{enumerate}
Applying \eqref{eq:Phi-bound}--\eqref{eq:Phi-error} to both values and first
parameter derivatives gives uniform convergence on the rational parameter
box:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hlimit}
 H^\infty(\alpha,t,w,\delta)=
 \left(
 \frac1\alpha+\frac w{t^2}+\delta,
 \frac w{t^3}+\delta,
 \frac{3w}{t^4}+3\delta,
 \frac{4w}{t^5}+4\delta
 \right).
\end{equation}
The value error is $O(e+x/e)$ and the derivative error is $O(e+x)$.
The xi contribution from \eqref{eq:derivative-tower} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Slimit}
 S^\infty=(-2,-1,-2,-2).
\end{equation}

The estimates in this section are finite-dimensional and elementary.  Their
two Fubini orientations, derivative identities, parameter segments, and
remote-boundary constants are all kernel-checked in the accompanying Lean
modules.

 3F_1-F_2=\frac{3w(t-1)}{t^4}-1,

\section{Jacobi factors and finite-free convolution}\label{sec:finitefree}

For $s>0$ put
\[
 Q_{U,V}^{(s)}(X)={}_2F_1\!\left(
 \begin{matrix}-d,U\\V\end{matrix};\frac{sX}{U}\right).
\]
Term-by-term comparison of the terminating series gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:concrete-mmp-factorization}
 p_F=Q_{A,B}^{(1)}\boxconv Q_{C,D}^{(D)}
\end{equation}
in the ascending constant-term-one convention.  Appendix
\ref{app:mmp-specialization} displays the coefficient calculation and checks
every hypothesis of the cited MMP propositions for these particular
parameters.  The literature uses the monic descending elementary-symmetric
convention.  If
$p^\vee(X)=X^dp(1/X)/p(0)$, direct coefficient algebra gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reversal-convolution}
 (p\boxconv q)^\vee=p^\vee\boxconv q^\vee.
\end{equation}
Reversal reciprocates positive roots and preserves strict logarithmic mesh.

Order roots as $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d>0$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lmesh}
 \lmesh(p)=\min_{1\le j<d}\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_{j+1}}\ge1.
\end{equation}
The inequalities $A\ge B+d$, $C\ge D+d$, and $B,D>0$ identify both factors
in \eqref{eq:concrete-mmp-factorization} with positive rescalings of Jacobi
polynomials having parameters greater than $-1$.  Thus each has degree $d$
and $d$ simple positive roots.  After the explicit reversal adapter, MMP
Proposition~2.7(iii) preserves nonnegative real-rootedness under
multiplicative convolution, while Proposition~2.17 in the pinned v3
convention gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lmesh-mmp}
 \lmesh(p\boxconv q)\ge\lmesh(p).
\end{equation}
For $d\ge2$, the first factor has logarithmic mesh strictly greater than one,
so \eqref{eq:lmesh-mmp} makes the convolution simple.  Proposition~2.7(iii)
puts all of its roots in $[0,\infty)$, and its constant term is one, so zero
is not a root.  The cases $d=0,1$ are immediate from the constant and linear
coefficients.  Finally \eqref{eq:concrete-mmp-factorization}, rather than an
assumption about $p_F$, transports this conclusion to the concrete
${}_3F_2$ comparison.

The Jacobi roots are localized without using Holland's inapplicable gap
hypothesis.  Transport the standard Jacobi matrix from $[-1,1]$ to the
positive-root variable.  Direct diagonal and off-diagonal estimates give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobi-local}
 U\ge V+d,\quad V\ge32d
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \operatorname{roots}(q_{U,V})\subset
 [V-8\sqrt{Vd},V+8\sqrt{Vd}].
\end{equation}
The constant $8$ is a Gershgorin consequence of a diagonal displacement at
most $4\sqrt{Vd}$ and adjacent off-diagonal row sum at most
$4\sqrt{Vd}$.

Before using the sharper product constant, the wedge and parameter box give
the non-circular estimates
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coarse-parameter}
 n\le B\le3n,\quad n/2\le D\le2n,\quad
 A\ge8B,\quad C\ge D+d,\quad B,D\ge256d.
\end{equation}
Theorem~1.6 of MSS bounds the maximum root of a multiplicative convolution
by the product of maximum roots.  Apply it both to the original polynomials
and to their reversals.  The reciprocal application requires positive lower
endpoints.  The preliminary geometry supplies them without circularity:
from $B,D\ge256d$,
\[
 \sqrt{Bd}\le B/16,\qquad \sqrt{d/D}\le1/16,
\]
and hence
\[
 B-8\sqrt{Bd}\ge B/2>0,\qquad
 1-8\sqrt{d/D}\ge1/2>0.
\]
These two strict inequalities are explicit arguments of the typed MSS
interface and are proved in Lean before that interface is invoked.  If $u$
and $v$ are the factor deviations from $B$
and $1$, then
\begin{align}
 |(B+u)(1+v)-B|
 &\le8\sqrt{Bd}+8B\sqrt{d/D}
       +64\sqrt{Bd}\sqrt{d/D}\nonumber\\
 &\le(12+8\sqrt6)\sqrt{Bd}.
 \label{eq:product-localization}
\end{align}
Thus every root, and by interlacing every critical point, lies in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:localization}
 |y_0-B|\le C_{\rm loc}\sqrt{Bd},\qquad
 C_{\rm loc}=12+8\sqrt6<32.
\end{equation}
The safe preliminary constant is $K_{\rm pre}=256$, not $32$; the latter
would allow a negative lower endpoint in the ratio-free second factor.

\section{Terminating hypergeometric ODE}\label{sec:ode}

For $0\le m\le d$, let $g_m$ be the shifted terminating polynomial with
parameters
\[
 (m-d,A+m,C+m;B+m,D+m).
\]
Its coefficient ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-ratio}
 \frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}
 =\frac{D(k+m-d)(k+A+m)(k+C+m)}
 {AC(k+1)(k+B+m)(k+D+m)}.
\end{equation}
Cross multiplication is valid for the terminating finite producer under the
positive parameter hypotheses.  With $\vartheta=y_0\,\dd/\dd y_0$, the
coefficient identity gives the shifted Euler equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler-ode}
 \vartheta(\vartheta+B+m-1)(\vartheta+D+m-1)g_m
 =\frac{D}{AC}y_0
 (\vartheta+m-d)(\vartheta+A+m)(\vartheta+C+m)g_m.
\end{equation}
The derivative-shift identity identifies $g_m$ with $p_F^{(m)}$ up to a
nonzero constant.  Expanding \eqref{eq:euler-ode} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-term}
 P_{3,m}T_{m+3}+P_{2,m}T_{m+2}
 +P_{1,m}T_{m+1}+P_{0,m}T_m=0,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tk}
 T_k=\frac{y_0^kp_F^{(k)}(y_0)}{p_F(y_0)}.
\end{equation}

For compact formulas, set
\begin{align*}
 a&=1-y_0/A,\\
 b_m&=B+m-y_0+(d-1-2m)y_0/A,\\
 c_m&=(d-m)(1+m/A)y_0,\\
 \epsilon_p&=(C-D)/C,\\
 \beta_m&=A(2m+1-d)-d(2m+1)\\
 &\hspace{2.2em}+3m^2+3m+1,\\
 \Gamma_m&=m(m-d)(m+A).
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align}
 P_{3,m}&=a+\epsilon_p y_0/A=(AC-Dy_0)/(AC),\nonumber\\
 P_{2,m}&=b_{m+1}+(D+m)a
 +\epsilon_p(y_0/A)(A-d+3+3m),\nonumber\\
 P_{1,m}&=c_{m+1}+(D+m)b_m+\epsilon_p(y_0/A)\beta_m,
 \label{eq:Pcoeffs}\\
 P_{0,m}&=(D+m)c_m+\epsilon_p(y_0/A)\Gamma_m
 =\frac{Dy_0(A+m)(C+m)(d-m)}{AC}.\nonumber
\end{align}
The finite coefficient producer, derivative shift, Euler ODE, and all four
closed forms are proved in Lean.  Mathematica independently returned four
zero coefficient differences and verified every $0\le m\le d$ for test
degrees $5,8,11$.

\section{Critical-point derivative radius}\label{sec:radius}

At a critical point of $p_F$, $T_0=1$ and $T_1=0$.  On the localized
interval \eqref{eq:localization}, exact parameter inequalities give,
uniformly for $0\le m\le d-2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence-bounds}
 P_{2,m}\ge n/8,\quad |P_{3,m}|\le2,\quad
 |P_{1,m}|\le C_1(n\sqrt{Bd}+nd),\quad
 0\le P_{0,m}\le C_0n^2d.
\end{equation}
The constants may be taken as $C_0=48$ and $C_1<96$ after the safe geometric
thresholds are imposed.  Note that $\epsilon_p\to1/2$; every occurrence in
\eqref{eq:Pcoeffs} remains in the main recurrence.

Choose $R=K_r\sqrt{Bd}$ with $K_r=4096$ and put
\[
 \mathcal M=\max_{0\le j\le d}\frac{|T_j|}{R^j}.
\]
If $\mathcal M>1$, choose a maximizing index $k$.  Since $T_0=1$ and
$T_1=0$, we have $k\ge2$.  Use \eqref{eq:four-term} with $m=k-2$, isolate
the $P_{2,k-2}T_k$ term, and divide by
$P_{2,k-2}R^k\mathcal M$.  Every other normalized neighbor is at most one
by the global definition of $\mathcal M$; when $k=d$, the higher neighbor is
$T_{d+1}=0$ by termination.  The constant, lower-order, and $P_3$ coefficient
budgets are strict fractions whose sum is below one.  Hence
$|T_k|/(R^k\mathcal M)<1$, contradicting maximality.  We obtain:

\begin{proposition}[Critical-point radius]\label{prop:radius}
At every critical point $y_0$ of $p_F$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radius}
 \max_{1\le k\le d}
 \left|\frac{y_0^kp_F^{(k)}(y_0)}{p_F(y_0)}\right|^{1/k}
 \le K_r\sqrt{Bd},\qquad K_r=4096.
\end{equation}
Separately, $T_0=1$.
\end{proposition}

The factors $y_0^k$ and the absence of $1/k!$ in \eqref{eq:radius} are
essential.  A previous manuscript transcription deleting the former and
inserting the latter was detected by numerical equation regression; the
recurrence and Cauchy tail use exactly \eqref{eq:Tk}.

\section{Complex Hermite--Genocchi residual}\label{sec:residual}

Let $E_F(z)$ be the logarithm of the exact xi/model coefficient ratio on the
positive parameter branch.  The six matches give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E-nodes}
 E_F(0)=E_F(1)=\cdots=E_F(5)=0.
\end{equation}
Six differentiations yield
\begin{align}
 E_F^{(6)}(z)={}&h^{(6)}(n+z)+\psi^{(5)}(B+z)
 -\psi^{(5)}(n+1/2+z)-\psi^{(5)}(A+z)\nonumber\\
 &+\psi^{(5)}(D+z)-\psi^{(5)}(C+z).
 \label{eq:E6-exact}
\end{align}
Pair the $B-C$ and $D-(n+1/2)$ terms before applying the polygamma series.
Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Omega}
 \Omega=\{z\in\C:\dist(z,[0,d])\le2\rho\},
 \qquad \rho=K_r\sqrt{Bd}.
\end{equation}
The explicit wedge constant ensures
\begin{equation}\label{eq:domain-containment}
 d+2\rho\le\eta n
\end{equation}
for a fixed small $\eta$.  Therefore $n+\Omega$ stays inside the inner xi
sector and every straight segment in the paired polygamma differences stays
in $\Re z\ge cn$.

The absolutely convergent series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:polygamma-series}
 \psi^{(5)}(z)=120\sum_{k\ge0}(z+k)^{-6},
 \qquad \Re z>0,
\end{equation}
the paired mean-value estimates, and \eqref{eq:derivative-tower} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E6}
 \sup_{z\in\Omega}|E_F^{(6)}(z)|
 \le\frac{C_{B6}}{n^5\log(n+2)}.
\end{equation}
Here $C_{B6}$ is a fixed explicit constant determined by the chosen sector
and parameter box.  It is kept symbolic in the effectivity ledger rather
than inferred from numerical samples.

We now state the complex interpolation lemma in the exact form used.

\begin{lemma}[Six-node complex Hermite--Genocchi]\label{lem:HG}
Let $U\subset\C$ be open and convex, let $f$ be holomorphic on $U$, and let
$z_0,\ldots,z_5,z\in U$ with their convex hull contained in a set on which
$|f^{(6)}|\le M$.  If $f(z_j)=0$ for $0\le j\le5$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HG}
 |f(z)|\le\frac{M}{720}\prod_{j=0}^{5}|z-z_j|.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Define the Newton remainders recursively.  Apply the complex line-segment
FTC at each stage.  Six repetitions express the final divided difference as
an integral of $f^{(6)}$ over the standard six-simplex.  Under the
stick-breaking map from $[0,1]^6$, the Jacobian weights are
\[
 (1-u_0)^5(1-u_1)^4(1-u_2)^3(1-u_3)^2(1-u_4).
\]
Their nested integral is
\[
 \frac16\cdot\frac15\cdot\frac14\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac12\cdot1
 =\frac1{720}.
\]
Convexity keeps every integration point in the derivative-bound set.
Multiplying the divided difference by the six Newton factors gives
\eqref{eq:HG}.
\end{proof}

Apply the lemma to $f=E_F$ and $z_j=j$.  The wedge may be enlarged so that
$\rho\ge d$.  Projection of $z\in\Omega$ to $[0,d]$ then gives
$|z-j|\le3\rho$ for $0\le j\le5$.  Hence
\begin{align}
 \sup_{z\in\Omega}|E_F(z)|
 &\le\frac{C_{B6}(3\rho)^6}{720n^5\log(n+2)}\nonumber\\
 &\le C_\ast\frac{d^3}{n^2\log(n+2)}.
 \label{eq:exponent}
\end{align}
The last line uses $B\le3n$ and
$\rho^6=K_r^6(Bd)^3$.  This is the only point at which the exponent three on
$d$ is created.

Set $c_F(z)=e^{E_F(z)}$.  On the chosen branch, \eqref{eq:E-nodes} implies
$c_F(0)=\cdots=c_F(5)=1$.  Increasing the absolute wedge constant in
\eqref{eq:wedge} makes the right side of \eqref{eq:exponent} small enough
that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplier-close}
 \sup_{z\in\Omega}|c_F(z)-1|\le1.
\end{equation}

\section{Multiplier stability and final assembly}\label{sec:assembly}

\begin{lemma}[Finite multiplier stability]\label{lem:stability}
Let $p(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_jy^j$ have $d$ simple positive zeros and
$p(0)\ne0$.  Let
$\Omega_r=\{z:\dist(z,[0,d])\le2r\}$, and suppose $c$ is holomorphic near
$\Omega_r$, real at $0,\ldots,d$, with
\[
 c(0)=\cdots=c(4)=1,\qquad c(d)>0,\qquad
 \sup_{\Omega_r}|c-1|\le\epsilon<16.
\]
If every critical point $y$ of $p$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stability-radius}
 \left|\frac{y^kp^{(k)}(y)}{p(y)}\right|\le r^k,
 \qquad0\le k\le d,
\end{equation}
then $P(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}p_jc(j)y^j$ has $d$ distinct positive zeros.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Newton interpolation of the finite data gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newton-multiplier}
 \frac{P(y)}{p(y)}
 =\sum_{k=0}^{d}\frac{\Delta^kc(0)}{k!}
 \frac{y^kp^{(k)}(y)}{p(y)}.
\end{equation}
Cauchy's estimate on discs of radius $2r$ gives
$|\Delta^kc(0)|/k!\le\epsilon/(2r)^k$.  The five displayed matches make the
terms $1\le k\le4$ vanish.  At every critical point,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geometric-tail}
 \left|\frac{P(y)}{p(y)}-1\right|
 \le\epsilon\sum_{k=5}^{d}2^{-k}
 <\frac\epsilon{16}<1.
\end{equation}
Thus $P$ and $p$ have the same nonzero sign at every critical point.  They
also agree in sign at zero, and $c(d)>0$ preserves the sign for sufficiently
large positive $y$.  The simple roots of $p$ give alternating signs at its
$d-1$ critical points and the two exterior points.  The intermediate-value
theorem places a sign-changing zero of $P$ in each intervening interval.
Those intervals are disjoint, so the zeros are distinct.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
For $d\le5$, the six exact coefficient matches already identify the whole
transformed polynomial with its comparison.  Let $d\ge6$.  Apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:stability} with $p=p_F$, $c=c_F$, and $r=\rho$.  Simplicity
and positivity come from Section~\ref{sec:finitefree};
Proposition~\ref{prop:radius} gives \eqref{eq:stability-radius}; and
\eqref{eq:multiplier-close} gives $\epsilon\le1<16$.  Hence the transformed
actual polynomial has $d$ distinct positive zeros.

The exact coefficient normalization has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:identify-Jensen}
 P(y)=\frac{\J^{d,n}(-y/S)}{\gamma(n)},
 \qquad S=BR_1>0,
\end{equation}
where $R_1$ is the positive first coefficient ratio on the constructed
branch.  The change $y=-SX$ takes distinct positive roots of $P$ to distinct
negative roots of $\J^{d,n}$.  This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main} above the
eventual analytic threshold.  Put
\[
 K_{\rm finite}=N_0^2(N_0+2)+1.
\]
If $0\le n<N_0$ and $d\ge1$, then
\[
 n^2\log(n+2)\le n^2(n+1)
 <N_0^2(N_0+2)<K_{\rm finite}\le K_{\rm finite}d^3.
\]
Consequently the wedge antecedent is empty below the cutoff once
$K\ge K_{\rm finite}$.  This is the finite absorption step; it invokes no
unproved finite computation.  Its arithmetic implication is also checked
by the Lean theorem
\code{not\_twoThirdsWedge\_finiteCutoffAbsorption}.
\end{proof}
